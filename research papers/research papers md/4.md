### **Abstract** 

The latency requirements, fragmented regulations, and ongoing risk of fraud are characteristic of modern payment networks. However, in the majority of production systems, the detection of fraud, compliance and routing remain loosely coupled functions that are not very scalable or resilient in multibank and crossborder contexts. Recent solutions based on AI enhance individual elements of fraud detection or compliance checks but do not do so on a joint basis in terms of risk, regulation, and implementation. This creates a severe void at the orchestration tier where real-time choices need to trade competing operational constraints. This paper presents a single AI-based orchestration framework of payments that involves a combination of federated fraud risk modeling, compliance-conscious admissibility screening, and adaptive routing reinforcement learning. The main innovation here is that the routing decisions are informed by probabilistic fraud and compliance intelligence and not postprocesses. Federated learning can be used to perform cross-bank risk modeling without the need to share raw data, and a lightweight blockchain-based audit layer provides verifiable accountability with minimum overhead. The results of experimental testing on large-scale synthetic transaction streams indicate that the proposed framework reduces the average settlement latency from 1290–1420 ms (baseline routing strategies) to 1050 ms, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 18–26%, while also reducing the route failure rate by more than 20% and compliance violations by approximately 45%, in comparison with rule-based routing, centralized AI fraud systems and fixed compliance baselines. The recall of fraud is 5–9% better than that of independent bank-level learning. These findings show that regulation-sensitive adaptive orchestration can be not only scaled but also necessary to create next-generation payment infrastructures that are resilient and reliable.

### **1 Introduction** 

The globalization of digital finance has accelerated payment processing as a back-office part of operations to a mission-critical real-time decision system. The contemporary transactions are becoming highly cross-bank, cross-settlement rail, and cross-regulatory boundaries, with each set of operations, security, and compliance imperatives. The increased transactions of today and the fast increasing rate of cross-border payments have necessitated the existence of orchestration layers in remittance of payments between nations or regions. The orchestration layer must conduct a due diligence task on the payment that is being made but they should also put into place suitable safety measures to both parties in the deal. These added complexities cannot be accommodated by traditional payment models which traditionally are based on fixed routing logic and independent verification modules. 

Due to this constraint, the requirement to develop smart orchestration tools which react and learn as the different forms in which the transactions will further evolve and change alongside increased demands on them and their network environment is now necessary besides the need to incorporate compliance and security requirements of an organization into their decision making processes. Artificial intelligence is transforming this aspect since it equips organisations with the power to encompass routing optimization as well as fraud detection without failing at regulatory demands by being a single, large, and scalable, automated, layer of payment processing control that can be applied across the entire scope of the global payment environment. 

#### **1.1 Background and motivation** 

Payments are now transferred from banks to banks in a multirail environment and connected through very different channels, presenting varying degrees of latency, liquidity, compliance and risk exposure [1]. Additionally, user expectations have changed dramatically. Moreover, users and organizations require the ability to make payments in near real-time (i.e., 24/7), track and receive payments anytime, anywhere, regardless of the currency used or country of origin. As a result, demand for payment systems has surged; thus, the levels of payment orchestration are now required to support real-time interactions among global payment providers (banks), payment rails and the reliance on compliance testing to ensure regulatory and tax compliance. Traditional payment processing models, which are primarily based on hardcoded algorithm routing logic and rulesetbased decision-making systems, have been unable to manage the required level of complexity [2]. Security represents another complication of this scenario. With respect to financial crimes (such as Fraud), there are far more sophisticated ways of exploiting the speed of each transaction, the anonymity of payment transactions around the world and the fragmented control systems surrounding those transactions. In addition, traditional fraud detection systems, which tend to be created using data from internal and private institutional data archives (Data Silos), do not provide the necessary level of comprehensive visibility across the globe and fail to identify new fraud patterns across banks and geographies [3, 4]. It needs to become a smart, dynamic control layer capable of learning through the experience of transactions, reacting to real-time risk indicators, and actively choosing the best settlement routes while being designed to be compliant. With proper application, artificial intelligence provides an opportunity to respond to these needs on a large scale [5].

#### **1.2 Problem statement** 

Although there has been a vast improvement in digital payment infrastructure, the existing multibank payment orchestration systems have several structural constraints. First, routing decisions are largely determined beforehand and are based on hard-coded priority policies or cost tables that fail to respond to changing network conditions, liquidity limits, or other indicators of risk. This kind of rigidity usually leads to a non optimal settlement time, a higher transaction failure rate, and unnecessary operational costs [6]. 

Second, the system of fraud detection is still institution-centric. Individual banks usually train and release their models, and the effect of this is the inability to have unified risk intelligence and slowness in identifying coordinated or cross-network fraud campaigns. Privacy and regulatory barriers that result in the absence of collaborative learning among banks are major detractions to resilience around the world in terms of fraud [7]. 

Third, compliance verification is often performed as a post routing process or a parallel process. Checks of regulations are enforced after a route to pay has already been selected which may lead to the rejection of the payment at the late stages, manual operations or regulatory violations in case the jurisdictional restrictions are not observed when routing decisions are taken [8]. Lastly, there has always been a problem with auditability. The existing logging systems are usually centralized and adaptable, and thus conflict resolution, forensic investigations, and audits of the regulators are rather challenging, particularly when cross-border scenarios are involved and more than one intermediary is involved [9]. 

All this culminates to a key problem which is, currently, the payment orchestration architecture lacks an embedded and dynamic intelligence layer which can optimize routing path, avert fraud, enforce compliance, and provide transparency in auditability at a global scale. 

#### **1.3 Research gap** 

Other more recent works have explored the personal information of intelligent payment processing, including machine learning-based fraud detection [10], Blockchain-based financial audit tests [11], and payment routing optimization models [12]. These efforts are however more or less disjointed. Single classifier models are also typically used in detecting fraud and are not combined with routing decisions. Blockchain solutions are founded on the soundness or permanence of settlement or the inability to change records as opposed to the instantaneous logic of orchestration. The discussed routing methods may be optimization-based and usually believe that the constraints are not dynamic and are not made on the basis of regulatory or risk-aware choices. Besides, available studies do not address multibank coordination because of privacy-preserving barriers in most of the studies. Despite the fact the concept of collaborative learning was already proposed in other areas, its application in cross-bank fraud detection and transaction risk modelling is relatively weak due to its so-called impact on data sharing and regulations [13]. This has resulted in ineffective intelligence of fraud across the globe. The other gap that can be identified is in the compliance treatment. The regulatory specifications are typically depicted as definite sets of rules or binary constraints. These descriptions are not characteristic of the complexity or jurisdiction specificity of real world regulations which are different by jurisdiction and vary over time [14].

#### **1.4 Proposed solution overview** 

To cope with the perceived limitations, the present paper suggests a self-driven multibank payment coordination system that is built upon an AI-powered system, which is intended to be utilized as an intelligent control layer in a heterogeneous network of banks. The suggested system also proposes adaptive decision-making during the orchestration level as opposed to routing, fraud detection, and compliance being treated as separate modules. 

The framework is centered on a reinforcement learning-based routing engine that acts as a representation of the payment network through a dynamic graph of payment corridors and banks. The routing agent does not need a fixed set of priorities but rather constantly learns the best transaction routes because of the settlement latency, success rates, liquidity, and risk results [15]. On the basis of real-time network behavior, routing decisions consequently change. The framework uses federated machine learning to detect fraud to ensure improved security without breaking the data privacy limits. Participant banks in the area train their fraud models using transaction data local to the bank and exchange encrypted updates to their models only. The model facilitates institutional learning, as well as adherence to data protection policies [16]. An AI-based compliance engine is directly integrated into the compliance logic. With natural language processing methods, regulatory documents and rules specific to jurisdiction are read with the goal of being translated into machine-processable constraints, which are used to make routing choices and authorization choices in real time. Cases are therefore assessed by considering their efficiency and risk, and they are assessed on the basis of whether they are compatible with regulations before they are executed. Finally, a layer of audit anchoring major orchestration events, including routing and compliance checks and risk screening, to an immutable store is implemented on blockchain. Transparency, nonrepudiation and verifiable audit trails are enforced by this design but do not impose too much overhead during transaction processing [17]. 

#### **1.5 Novelty of the work** 

The originality of the work does not exist in the single application of artificial intelligence or distributed ledger technologies but rather in the systematic approach to their combination into one payment orchestration architecture. In contrast to the current methodologies, the proposed framework considers payment orchestration as a decisionmaking problem under uncertainty, regulatory risk, and adversarial risk in a sequential manner. First, reinforcement learning applied to real-time and multibank payment routing is a break with respect to nondynamically and optimization-based routing policies. The proposed model learns on the basis of operational feedback, and as such, it constantly adapts to changing network conditions. Second, federated learning applied in cross-bank fraud detection allows risk intelligence to be obtained internationally without violating the privacy of institutional data, which all other existing models of centralized or siloed models do not offer. Third, the addition of a compliance engine, which is powered by AI, turns regulatory enforcement into a strict enforcement point, but a more dire method that has a proactive role in catalyzing transactions. 

Finally, the blockchain audit layer is closely connected with the AI decision pipeline, where intelligent-based decisions are transparent, traceable, and ready for regulators.

#### **1.6 Research contributions** 

The paper makes several substantive contributions to intelligent payment orchestration in world financial systems. First, it offers a single AI-enabled coordination model that provides collective responses to address transaction flow, fraud detection and regulatory requirements and auditability as opposed to the disjointed architectures typically implemented in current payment platforms. Second, it introduces a routing mechanism, which is based on reinforcement learning, and dynamically chooses the best multibank payment paths while simultaneously considering settlement latency, liquidity status, risk exposure, and jurisdiction-specific compliance constraints. Third, the research uses the federated machine learning approach for fraud detection, where the risk information of multiple banks can be collaboratively learned without violating privacy and regulatory confidentiality. Moreover, an artificial intelligence-based compliance engine is designed to read and implement heterogeneous regulatory specifications in real time and puts compliance awareness directly into orchestration choices, as opposed to compliance awareness being a postprocessing action. Lastly, the framework will have a built-in blockchain-based audit layer, which records essential orchestration events in a verifiable and unalterable format, therefore, forming the basis of transparency as well as dispute resolutions and audits by regulators. Combined, they form a scalable and adaptive payment orchestration paradigm which is suitable to offer secure, real-time, and compliance-aware financial transactions at the global scale.

### **2 Literature review** 

The history of digital payment systems has attracted long-standing interest because financial infrastructures are moving toward real-time, cross-border, and multirail transaction models. Initial research in this area focused mostly on enhancing settlement efficiency and cross-banking network interoperability, typically by standardizing protocols and harmonizing messages [18]. Although these efforts provided a basis for modern payment rails, they assumed mostly foreseeable flows of transactions and fixed conditions in the network. 

Later studies were extended into the area of optimization of payment routing, especially in corresponding banking and cross-border settlement networks. Some of the works represented payment routing as a cost minimization or latency reduction problem, and graph theoretic or linear optimization methods were used to find efficient transaction paths [19]. Despite showing quantifiable gains when used in controlled

assumptions, they cannot be scaled to real-world conditions of changing liquidity, network congestion or the changing constraints of regulatory conditions. Moreover, the routing decision is not usually coupled with security and compliance issues. 

The problem of fraud detection has been researched as an autonomous issue in digital finance. The conventional methods used were rule-based approaches and statistical benchmarks that were only effective in circumventing familiar fraud profiles [20]. As machine learning has increased in prominence, scholars have started to use supervised classifiers, such as ensemble models and neural networks, to model complicated transaction patterns [21]. 

Collaborative and distributed learning paradigms have started to become popular for resolving the problem of data fragmentation. Specifically, federated learning has been investigated as a privacy-sensitive scheme to train common models on decentralized sources of information [22]. There were earlier uses in the financial sector where it was proven to be practical in scoring credit and in fraud detection without revealing the actual transactions. Nevertheless, the majority of researchers are concerned with the accuracy of models and privacy guarantees, but they do not apply federated learning to real-time payment decision pipelines. 

Along with the progress in fraud detection, the automation of regulatory compliance has begun to receive increased attention. Financial regulations were broadened swiftly in scale and complexity to include antimoney laundering, counterterrorism financing, sanction enforcement and consumer data protection [23]. The initial compliance systems were based on manual compliance review and deterministic rule engines, which made them extremely costly to run and slow in processing transactions. Later, automated compliance systems were suggested to help simplify verification, although the frameworks thought of compliance as a detached validation step instead of a working constraint that impacted transaction routing [24]. 

More recent efforts have attempted to use compliance considerations in transaction processing by policy-based engines and rule sets that could be configured [25]. Although the solutions enhanced flexibility, they could not address the heterogeneity of regulations across jurisdictions. The encoding of rules at rest was also fragile when there was a frequent change in regulations. Consequently, compliance automation was operational in a reactive manner, coming in only once routing decisions had been made. 

Blockchain technology provides opportunities for transparency and trust in payment systems. Some of the papers investigated distributed ledgers in terms of cross-border settlements, reconciliation and auditability [26]. The blockchain-based solutions helped to overcome the problems that existed before and contributed to long-standing dispute questions, setting out the objective of the settlements and verifying whether postsettlement occurred. Nevertheless, most of the suggested systems have a high cost of latency and scalability overheads, which reduces their applicability to real-time orchestration [27]. In addition, blockchain was mostly used as a parallel ledger that was not related to the intelligence behind transaction decisions. 

The integration of blockchain with artificial intelligence in the financial sector has slowly developed, with an emphasis on secure data sharing, decentralized training of models, and automated compliance reporting [28]. These papers highlight the complementary advantages of AI and distributed ledgers without suggesting tightly integrated architectures in which blockchain directly records AI-initiated orchestration events. The learning algorithm known as reinforcement learning has increased in popularity as a model of sequential decision-making in uncertain contexts and has been used in networking and resource allocation, as well as in traffic management [29]. Financially, reinforcement learning has been used to optimize portfolios, trade and credit risk management [30]. Only a few studies have investigated its use in payment routing, most of which have simulated models that do not consider regulatory and fraud-related issues [31]. These models are flexible but not grounded in the workability of multibank transaction ecosystems. 

The problem of scaling intelligent payment systems in heterogeneous payment rails has also been analyzed. Research examining local instant payment systems and international networks on interoperability has revealed the operational complexity posed by conflicting settlement cycles, message standards and liquidity systems [32]. The proposed solutions were primarily grounded on the middleware abstractions and protocol translation layers that increased connectivity but not on the adaptive intelligence. 

In relation to the system, audit requirements and observability have been getting priority. The financial institutions will have to provide a clear description of the choices taken in the transactions, the risk assessment and compliance tests to the regulators [33]. The use of explainable AI techniques to improve the transparency of financial models has been proposed, although it is not currently applied on real-time payment orchestration [34]. Most orchestration frameworks lack a consistent system upon which it is possible to trace the rationales of decisions by routing, measuring risks and enforcing compliance. 

The present-day polls of the intelligent financial infrastructure emphasize the fact that extensive designs that transcend the individual optimizations are required [35]. These writings need to have the new payment systems combined with adaptive routing, cooperative security intelligence and regulatory awareness on the same plane of control. However, the real-life examples of the practical application of all three dimensions simultaneously are few. 

Some of these designs have proposed hybrid systems, which include machine learning and policy-based orchestration engines [36]. Even though they do not have a bad direction, these approaches are often centered on centralized learning and predetermined compliance environments. They can be used only to a certain degree since the banking environment of the global community does not provide them with privacy protection due to the lack of collaboration that guarantees privacy and the active understanding of the rules. 

The issue of data privacy and governance has also been cited as one of the major limitations. Government regulations already limit the cross-border sharing of data and the use of central analytics is becoming harder to perform [37]. Even though federated and decentralized learning-based models are a way of choice, their implementation in payment systems that depend on latency presents new issues that should raise concerns of synchronization, convergence, and robustness [38]. 

To add to the performance requirements, a development of real time payment systems has also aggravated the level of performance requirements. In an investigation of instant payment infrastructures, both hard latency budgets and high availability requirements

have been documented [39]. The smart orchestration system must therefore operate within severe-time limitations with minimal opportunities of sustain heavyweight analytics or in validation of stages. 

Despite these advances that have occurred, a discontinuity that persists in the literature exists. The detection of fraud, routing optimization, compliance automation and auditability are generally handled as separate issues. Integration is not tight even in cases where several components are integrated with little interaction of decision layers [40]. This leads to a situation where system-wide optimization is difficult to achieve. 

Recent conceptualizations promote learning-based orchestration of financial systems [41]. The high-level architectures are described by these proposals, but the details of the implementation are often missing, as are experimental validations and performance analyses using realistic workloads. Their performance cannot be re-evaluated, which limits their impact. 

Moreover, few studies have directly considered the global nature of current payment ecosystems. Other simplified or neglected factors are cross-jurisdictional compliance, heterogeneous risk profiles, and multibank coordination [42]. The latter is especially prominent in the field of studies on AI-based decision-making, where regulatory constraints are often considered to be alien filters instead of inherent components of the learning process. 

Recent work has also been affected by the growing interest in secure-by-design financial architectures [43]. There is increased focus on ensuring security and compliance as part of system design as opposed to overlaying them on top. Nevertheless, there is still no attempt to operationalize this principle into real-time orchestration frameworks. 

To conclude, the current literature can offer useful information regarding individual elements of intelligent payment systems but does not offer a comprehensive solution. Few studies have been conducted concurrently in adaptive routing, privacy-preserving fraud intelligence, compliance-based decision-making, and verifiable audit trials [44]. This disparity is further exacerbated by the fact that payment ecosystems are becoming larger in size across the world and regulatory demands are increasing. 

Given these observations, the current work attempts to fill this gap by proposing a single AI-based multibank payment coordination system that effectively coordinates these capabilities into a single, scalable, and regulator-compliant system [45].

The authoritarian gap is covered in the proposed work, as it incorporates these dimensions into a unified adaptive orchestration architecture. #### **3.2 Data construction and feature engineering** 

A multibank payment orchestration framework evaluation requires transaction data, which record regulatory diversity, operational realism, and adversarial behavior and are reproducible and privacy preserving. The real-world logs of the interbank payment process are not available since they are confidential and regulated, so the proposed research applies a controlled strategy of synthetic and hybrid data construction. It is not aimed at copying proprietary datasets but rather at producing streams of transactions that accurately represent the statistical and structural characteristics of the world payment systems when under different load, risk and compliance conditions. 

Along with the synthetic generation of transactions, the statistical parameterization of the dataset is guided by publicly available and open-source references, such as reports of global payment systems, disclosures of regulatory authorities, and industry standards. In particular, the underlying sampling processes and operational constraints are calibrated with specific open statistics on distributions of transaction volumes at the cross-border, utilization of cross-border corridors, and ranges of settlement latency and reported fraud prevalence, not with the ingestion of raw records of transactions [46–49]. This stratagem mix maintains confidentiality and reproducibility, and the simulated environment behaves structurally reasonably as a payment system. This means that the results of the evaluation can be viewed as indicators of the robustness of the system at the level of orchestration and not as indicators of performance against any proprietary banking dataset. 

#### **_3.2.1 Synthetic transaction stream generation_** 

A transaction dataset D = {xi}i = 1 N is generated, where each transaction xi follows the formal definition introduced in (1). The amounts of transactions, ai, are sampled to be a combination of log-normal distributions to reflect the presence of low-value and highvalue distributions of retail and corporate transfers, respectively. Weighted sampling of sender receiver corridor pairs (cs,cr) is used to reflect realistic patterns of cross-border and domestic traffic. The rails to which a candidate payment is conditionally assigned depend on the feasibility of the corridors, and transactions are only considered on the rails that are operationally feasible. Liquidity at the bank level is modeled as a limited stochastic dynamic that is updated at discrete time steps, with a realistic time variation in the feasibility of a route, and does not disrupt the learning process. Parameterized mechanisms are used to inject fraud events, e.g., bursts in transaction velocity, anomalous amount deviations, and repeated routing patterns that permit the prevalence and severity of fraud to be manipulated in an orderly way by experimentation. All the parameters of the generation, such as random seeds, distribution limits, and rates of fraud injection, are fixed and documented to provide perfect reproducibility via experimental runs.

#### **_3.2.4 Data generation and ground truth construction_** 

To ensure full experimental reproducibility, all synthetic and hybrid transaction data were generated using a deterministic simulation process with fixed random seeds. A total of 300,000 transaction records were synthesized to emulate multi-bank payment corridors operating under heterogeneous liquidity and network conditions. 

The value of transactions was sampled according to a log-normal distribution with μ = 4.0 mean and σ = 1.1 standard deviation, which is a representation of the heavy-tailed property of a real-life payment system. Liquidity indices were also selected as Beta(2.5, 4.0) whereby network load was selected as a uniform distribution in [0.2, 1.0] in order to model constrained settlement capacity and different levels of congestion respectively. Time slot hours were placed under the time qualities of 24 h. Each of the simulations was executed with a given random seed ( seed = 2025) such that they can be re-executed deterministically. 

Fraud risk scores were modeled as a nonlinear, weighted, and normalized transaction amount, inverse liquidity index, and network load where there was an additive, Gaussian noise term to represent stochastic behavior variation. When the score in the fraud risk transaction was above a cutoff value τ f = 0.72, a transaction was considered fraudulent and was incorporated into the binary fraud ground truth upon which federated training was executed. 

Compliance ground truth was defined through deterministic admissibility rules reflecting jurisdictional and risk-based constraints. Transactions originating from highregulation regions (EU and US) with amounts exceeding USD 1,000,000 were labeled as non-admissible. In addition, any transaction with a fraud risk score above τ_f was also marked as compliance-invalid. These rule-based assignments produced categorical compliance labels used by the admissibility engine and routing controller. 

The complete dataset generation pipeline, together with all model implementations and experimental scripts, has been released as a minimal reproducibility package to enable independent validation of the reported results.

#### **3.7 Implementation details and reproducibility** 

The subsection is a summary of the implementation decisions, hyperparameter settings, and reproducibility guarding embraced in the entire proposed framework. Its main goal is that the experimental findings would be self-replicable under the same conditions and remain consistent throughout the process of the attack on the models of fraud, their screening, routing, and audit anchoring. 

#### **_3.7.1 System implementation environment_** 

Every aspect of the orchestration architecture was deployed with Python-based modular services to capture the deployment conditions in the real financial infrastructure. The models of machine learning and reinforcement learning were executed on the basis of regular numeric and deep learning libraries, whereas the simulation and orchestration logic were performed in an event-driven processing pipeline. A commodity computing environment was put under experimentation without having a special hardware accelerator; in this way, reported performance will not be dependent on proprietary infrastructure. 

Intermodule communication follows stateless interfaces, allowing fraud prediction, compliance screening, routing, and audit anchoring to be executed independently or composed into a unified pipeline. This modular design also supports partial reuse of components in alternative deployment settings. 

#### **_3.7.2 Hyperparameter configuration_** 

All the learning-related hyperparameters were fixed prior to experimentation and remained unchanged across the comparative evaluations. Fraud risk models employ a weighted binary cross-entropy loss, as defined in (6), with the classweight parameter α\alphaα selected to penalize false negatives more strongly than false positives. Federated learning was executed via a fixed number of communication rounds, local training epochs per round, and a constant learning rate, as summarized earlier in Table 3. 

In the case of adaptive routing, the reinforcement learning agent employs a constant discount factor 7, replay buffer capacity, minibatch size and target network update frequency. The schedule used in exploration was of monotonically decaying ε-greedy which was initialized with sufficient early exploration and decreased to stabilize convergence. Reward weights: αL, αC, αf, αc and αV were chosen to ensure an efficiency, risk, and compliance factor were equal across all the routing experiments to prevent tuning to outcomes. 

The compliance thresholds of τc and the fraud risk thresholds of τf were also predetermined to each run of the experiment. These thresholds were selected in a conservative manner and allowed to represent regulatory-first operation settings and not performance-optimized ones..

### **5 Discussion** 

The most noticeable tendency is that the increase in latency, compliance adherence, and routing stability was not achieved on its own; it only became a reality when fraud intelligence, regulatory admissibility and routing decisions were optimized under one orchestration loop. This finding confirms the original hypothesis of this study that modular optimization, in which fraud detection, compliance screening and routing are viewed as sequential or loosely coupled phases, introduces inefficiencies that become compounding with scale. Conversely, incorporating probabilistic risk indicators into the routing policy enabled the system to internalize trade-offs in accordance with the dynamics, which is why the violation rates, as well as route failures, were reduced. The smoother performance achieved under high transaction loads further indicates that adaptive decision-making removes threshold-based instability, which is an offset failure of the previously used nonpayment infrastructure. 

Baseline routing methods are evaluated under deployment-consistent assumptions. Centralized baselines assume full visibility of transaction states and incur coordination overhead, whereas the proposed framework operates under federated fraud estimation and decentralized admissibility screening. Therefore, performance comparisons are based on realistic orchestration limitations, as opposed to the idealized centralized control, so that the reported improvement of latency, and compliance reports can be understood in the context of realistic deployment. 

Compared with the previous methods, the suggested framework is superior to the centralized AI-inspired fraud monitoring pipelines that presuppose unlimited data agglomeration and low regulatory heterogeneity [13, 31]. Although these systems have been reported to have good classification, they frequently fail to accommodate the realities of deployment, including cross-bank information isolation and geographical fragmentation. On the same note, the hard- and fixed-point compliance-based architectures minimize violations, however, at the expense of rejecting too many transactions and lower throughput [19, 40]. According to the results, probabilistic compliance modeling combined with routing decisions can provide a more balanced operational profile. With respect to routing, both strategies of static or cost-only optimization that have been reported in large payment platforms [26, 28] do not scale under liquidity pressure or regulatory contention. The routing policy proposed here on the basis of reinforcement 

Pendyala _et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence_ (2026) 6:427 

Page 41 of 44 

learning is more resilient because it reconsiders the possible paths constantly instead of following the preset heuristics. 

Theoretically, the results support the view that real-time financial systems can be modeled as constrained sequential decision processes as opposed to deterministic pipelines. The interplay between compliance risk and routing feasibility injects nonstationarity, which cannot be represented in the process of static optimization. In practice, this would imply that it would be beneficial to implement cloud-native payment platforms with adaptive intelligence implemented at the orchestration layer instead of implementing AI elements around control logic that already exist. A rather surprising but still insightful observation is the stabilizing nature of compliance-conscious gating on routing behavior: by discarding actions that cannot be made in the early stages of the learning process, the agent was able to converge more consistently, which implies that regulatory constraints can be used as inductive biases and not necessarily limiting conditions. 

Additionally, the experimental environment is a model of a limited set of payment rails and jurisdictions, which are not necessarily representative of the diversity of the payment ecosystem around the world. Future work should include validation on the basis of anonymized regulatory sandboxes or consortium-based testbeds and extrapolation of the framework to new payment instruments and settlement technologies. 

More generally, this publication addresses the current trends of responsible AI in financial infrastructure, in which performance, compliance, and transparency have ceased to be rival goals and instead become design constraints. The study will also add to the emerging literature supporting system-level, as opposed to model-focused, innovation in global payment systems by showing that adaptive intelligence and regulatory rigor can coexist in a single orchestration. 

### **6 Conclusion and future scope** 

This paper aims to bridge a pragmatic gap in the design of large-scale payment infrastructure, i.e., the absence of coordinated intelligence among fraud risk, regulatory compliance, and real-time routing. These functions were not considered independent or sequential processes, but through the study, an integrated orchestration framework was proposed where learning, decision-making, and auditability work within a common control loop. The primary contribution is that the efficiency of payments, the soundness of regulations, and the resilience of operations do not have to be sacrificed to each other when intelligence at the system level is planned. This change in view is significant, as it redefines payment orchestration as a dynamic decision problem but not a fixed execution pipeline. 

The federated fraud modeling scheme minimizes false negatives without using centralized data aggregation, a benefit that is difficult to achieve in multibank settings where privacy and regulation limits are strictly enforced. Admissibility screening of compliance reduces the rate of violation without oversuppressing transactions, as is the case with strict rule-based solutions. Second, most importantly, the adaptive routing policy was able to achieve a consistent and systematic reduction in the settlement latency and route success on dynamic liquidity and congestion, suggesting that learning-based orchestration is able to react to operational stress in a manner that is not possible with the application of static heuristics. When these results are combined, they indicate that it is 

Pendyala _et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence_ (2026) 6:427 

Page 42 of 44 

not individual optimization that has meaningful performance gains but the interaction between the risk, regulation and execution logic. 

Moreover, the scope of this study is limited by its experimental setting. The testing is based on artificially created streams of transactions scaled by references to open sources and cannot scale well to the interbank traffic it will experience in practice. The network model also indicates a finite number of payment rails and jurisdictions, and it can be expanded to a variety of more diverse regulatory and infrastructural settings. 

It would challenge its flexibility in changing infrastructure to extend the framework to include emerging settlement technologies, including instant cross-border rails or tokenized payment instruments. It is also possible to conduct further expressive learning paradigm studies such as multiagent coordination across banks or adaptive policy learning to regulatory drift. Beyond this, the work provides a route to faster, cheaper and more fundamentally risk aware, regulation-conscious and accountable payment systems that are likely to become the hallmark of the next generation of international financial infrastructure.
