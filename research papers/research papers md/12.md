### **Abstract** 

The digital transformation of public services has created an urgent demand for intelligent question-answering (QA) systems that can deliver both accurate and context-aware responses. While Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) enables efficient finetuning of large language models (LLMs) for domain-specific tasks, it struggles to capture sparse yet critical personalized information–such as residency status, age, or contribution history–that appears in only a small fraction of training data. This paper proposes SimLoRA, a novel parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that integrates the SimAM (A Simple, Parameter-Free Attention Module) 3D attention mechanism into the LoRA framework. SimLoRA dynamically weights low-rank updates based on neuronal distinctiveness, amplifying the contribution of informative features while suppressing generic patterns, all without introducing additional trainable parameters. We evaluate our approach on a real-world citizen card QA dataset from Hangzhou, China, consisting of 70,000 QA pairs where 3% of queries contain personalized information. Furthermore, as an optional enhancement, applying Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm yields a 57.2% win rate against the SimLoRA baseline in realworld preference-based evaluation, producing responses better aligned with user expectations for public service scenarios. SimLoRA offers an effective, computationally efficient pathway for deploying personalized LLM-based QA systems in critical public service domains where accuracy and personalization are paramount. 

**Keywords** Large language models, Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, Low-rank adaptation, SimAM, Attention mechanism, Reinforcement learning from human feedback, Personalized question answering, Digital public services

Early-generation QA systems in public service domains relied predominantly on knowledge bases coupled with keyword matching techniques [2]. The advent of large language models (LLMs) has inaugurated a paradigm shift in conversational AI. In the context of public service QA, LLMs offer the promise of systems that truly understand citizen questions rather than just matching keywords, delivering responses that are both informative and contextually appropriate. 

Despite these advances, the implementation of LLMs in specialized domains such as digital citizenship presents two persistent challenges. First, computational constraints pose a significant barrier. Full-parameter fine-tuning on domain-specific data remains computationally prohibitive for most public sector institutions due to the billions of parameters involved [6].

While factually correct, these answers failed to address the specific circumstances embedded in user queries, undermining the personalization that citizens expect from modern digital services. Beyond the challenge of personalization, LLM-based systems exhibit another welldocumented limitation: the tendency to generate plausible but incorrect information, often termed “hallucination” [8]. More subtly, models may produce responses that, while factually accurate, feel mechanical or misaligned with user expectations regarding tone and helpfulness. These issues stem from training objectives that optimize for next-token prediction rather than human preference alignment, and from biases embedded in training corpora that may not reflect optimal interaction patterns for public service contexts. 

Recent research has explored attention mechanisms to enhance model focus on salient input features. SimAM (A Simple, Parameter-Free Attention Module) offers an elegant approach by computing 3D attention weights based on neuronal importance without introducing additional trainable parameters [9]. Drawing on neuroscientific principles that information-rich neurons exhibit distinctive activation patterns relative to their neighbors, SimAM assigns importance scores across both channel and spatial dimensions simultaneously. This parameter-free characteristic makes SimAM particularly attractive for integration with PEFT methods, as it enhances the representational capacity without undermining the parameter efficiency that makes LoRA attractive. 

Separately, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a powerful technique for aligning LLM outputs with human preferences [10]. By training a reward model on human preference data and optimizing the language model policy against this reward using algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), RLHF steers model behavior toward outputs that humans judge as helpful, honest, and harmless [11]. This approach has been central to the success of production dialogue systems, yet its application to specialized public service domains remains underexplored. 

In this paper, we address the aforementioned challenges by proposing SimLoRA, a novel lightweight fine-tuning method that integrates the SimAM attention into the LoRA framework. Unlike standard LoRA, which applies uniform low-rank updates across all features, SimLoRA dynamically weights updates based on the distinctiveness of features, thereby amplifying the learning of sparse but critical personalized information. Furthermore, we incorporate RLHF with PPO as an additional refinement step to align model outputs with citizen expectations regarding helpfulness, truthfulness, and tone.

### **2 Related work** 

This section reviews the three main research threads relevant to this work: parameterefficient fine-tuning methods with a focus on LoRA, attention mechanisms with an emphasis on SimAM, and reinforcement learning from human feedback for LLM alignment. We conclude by identifying the research gap that motivates our proposed SimLoRA method. 

#### **2.1 Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)** 

The emergence of large language models with billions of parameters has made full fine-tuning increasingly impractical for most researchers and practitioners. This has catalyzed extensive research into parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods that adapt pre-trained models to downstream tasks while updating only a small fraction of parameters. 

Adapter-based methods represent one prominent approach. Houlsby et al. [12] proposed inserting small trainable modules (adapters) between transformer layers. These adapters consist of a down-projection, a non-linearity, and an up-projection, with the original model parameters frozen. Although effective, adapters introduce additional latency during inference as a result of sequential processing through the adapter layers. Subsequent work has explored adapter placement optimization and parameter sharing strategies to reduce computational overhead [13]. 

Prompt tuning offers an alternative paradigm that learns soft commands in response to the input. Lester et al. [14] demonstrated that for large models, prompt tuning can achieve a performance comparable to full fine-tuning while updating only a tiny fraction of parameters. However, prompt tuning typically requires large model scales to be effective, with performance degrading significantly for models under 10 billion parameters. Liu et al. [15] extended this idea with P-Tuning v2, which incorporates deep prompt tuning across multiple layers. 

LoRA is founded on the hypothesis of low intrinsic dimensionality, which posits that pre-trained models have a low “intrinsic dimension” and can be adapted to new tasks by updating parameters within a low-rank subspace [7]. Concretely, for a pre-trained weight matrix, LoRA introduces two trainable low-rank matrices whose product approximates the weight update, while the original weights remain frozen. This design reduces trainable parameters by orders of magnitude, reduces memory footprint, enables fast task switching without reloading the base model, and incurs no inference latency. 

Recent years have witnessed numerous extensions of LoRA. AdaLoRA [16] dynamically allocates parameter budgets across layers based on importance scores, but this introduces additional computational overhead and complexity. QLoRA [17] focuses on quantization for memory efficiency rather than feature selection. Delta-LoRA [18] explores updates of higher-rank, but this increases the parameter count. None of these approaches addresses the fundamental problem of feature prioritization during weight updates. 

Despite these advances, standard LoRA and its variants treat all features uniformly during weight updates, applying the same low-rank transformation across the entire feature space. This uniform treatment becomes limiting when training data contains sparse but critical signals–such as personalized user information in domain-specific queries–as the model struggles to prioritize these rare features over more frequent generic patterns.

It is worth noting that the inference efficiency of standard LoRA–enabled by static merging of _BA_ into _W_ 0 relies critically on the input-agnostic nature of its updates. While this property is desirable for deployment, it fundamentally limits the model’s ability to differentiate sparse, context-dependent features. Our proposed method, presented in Section 3, intentionally relaxes this constraint to achieve dynamic feature prioritization, at the cost of replacing static merging with online modulation. 

In addition to parameter-efficient tuning, domain-specific QA tasks often suffer from data scarcity due to the high cost of collecting and annotating domain data. To address this challenge, question generation techniques have been widely adopted to automatically construct high-quality training samples and enhance data diversity, thereby improving the robustness and generalization of fine-tuned models [19, 20]. 

#### **2.2 Attention mechanisms** 

Attention mechanisms have become fundamental components in deep neural networks, enabling models to focus on salient features while suppressing irrelevant information. The transformer architecture [21] introduced self-attention, which computes attention weights based on pairwise interactions between all positions in a sequence. This has proven to be remarkably effective for sequence modeling tasks. 

In computer vision, the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) [22] popularized the approach of sequentially applying channel and spatial attention. CBAM computes channel attention via global average and max pooling, followed by spatial attention using convolutional layers, generating separate 1D and 2D attention weights. However, this decomposition into separate dimensions may not capture the full 3D interdependencies across channels and spatial positions simultaneously. 

More recently, SimAM (A Simple Parameter-Free Attention Module) was proposed as an elegant alternative that directly infers 3D attention weights without introducing additional parameters [9]. Drawing inspiration from neuroscientific principles–specifically that information-rich neurons exhibit distinctive activation patterns compared to their neighbors and actively suppress surrounding neurons (spatial inhibition)–SimAM formulates an energy function to quantify the importance of each neuron. 

SimAM offers three key advantages: (1) parameter-free operation–it introduces no trainable parameters, preserving model efficiency; (2) true 3D attention–it simultaneously considers channel and spatial dimensions rather than decomposing them; and (3) neuroscientific grounding–its formulation derives from biological principles of visual processing. These characteristics make SimAM particularly attractive for integration with PEFT methods, as it can enhance feature discrimination without undermining the parameter efficiency that makes LoRA attractive. 

#### **2.3 Reinforcement learning from human feedback** 

Beyond fine-tuning domain-specific data, aligning LLM outputs with human preferences has emerged as a critical challenge [10]. Models optimized solely for next-token prediction may generate plausible but incorrect information (hallucinations), exhibit unwanted biases, or produce responses that feel mechanical rather than helpful. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) addresses these issues by incorporating human preference signals directly into model optimization [11].

RLHF typically proceeds in three phases. First, a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) model is obtained through standard supervised learning on the demonstration data. Second, a reward model is trained on human preference comparisons–given a prompt and two candidate responses, human annotators indicate which they prefer, and the reward model learns to assign higher scores to preferred responses using a pairwise ranking loss. Third, the SFT model (now acting as a policy) is fine-tuned to maximize the reward model scores while remaining close to the original SFT distribution to prevent mode collapse. 

The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm has become the standard choice for the reinforcement learning phase in RLHF. PPO is an on-policy actor-critic method that addresses stability challenges by constraining policy updates via a clipped surrogate objective, preventing excessively large updates that could destabilize training. For LLM alignment, PPO is typically augmented with a KL penalty term that discourages the updated policy from diverging too far from the SFT model, preserving general capabilities while steering toward human preferences. 

Despite its effectiveness, RLHF with PPO presents practical challenges: it requires maintaining four models (policy, reference policy, reward model, value network), is computationally intensive, and can be sensitive to hyperparameter choices. Recent alternatives such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [23] have emerged, but PPO remains the dominant approach in production systems due to its stability and empirical performance. 

#### **2.4 Summary and research gap** 

The literature reveals three relevant but disconnected threads: LoRA provides parameter-efficient adaptation to new domains; SimAM offers parameter-free 3D attention for feature enhancement; and RLHF with PPO enables alignment with human preferences. However, existing LoRA variants treat all features uniformly during adaptation, making them ill-suited for domains where critical information appears sparsely in training data– such as personalized user circumstances in digital citizen services. In contrast, while attention mechanisms like SimAM could theoretically help focus on such sparse signals, they have not been integrated into the LoRA adaptation process. 

This paper bridges the identified gaps by proposing SimLoRA, which integrates SimAM attention weighting directly into LoRA’s low-rank updates. This integration enables the model to amplify updates for features corresponding to user-specific information while maintaining parameter efficiency. Furthermore, we demonstrate that subsequent refinement of the RLHF further enhances response quality, producing outputs that are accurate and aligned with citizen expectations. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that combines attention-weighted low-rank adaptation with human preference alignment for domain-specific LLM fine-tuning in public service applications.

#### **3.1 Problem formulation and motivation**

Given a domain-specific training dataset _D_ = _{_ ( _xi, yi_ ) _}_ consisting of user queries _xi_ and target responses _yi_ , the goal is to adapt the model to generate accurate and personalized responses. However, in our digital citizen card scenario, only a small fraction (approximately 3%) of queries contain personalized context–such as residency status, contribution history, or family relationships–while the majority are generic. Standard LoRA applies uniform low-rank updates to all features, treating rare personalized signals and frequent generic patterns equally. This leads to models that default to generic responses when faced with queries that contain sparse but critical user-specific information. 

Our key insight is that the adaptation process should amplify updates for distinctive features while suppressing those that are common across many examples. To achieve this, we propose dynamically weighting low-rank updates using a parameter-free attention mechanism that measures the distinctiveness of each feature based on its statistical deviation from the channel mean. This allows the model to focus on informative patterns while suppressing generic ones, thereby improving personalization without sacrificing parameter efficiency.

#### **3.6 Implementation considerations** 

The integration of SimAM into LoRA is motivated by three considerations. First, SimAM is parameter-free, preserving LoRA’s parameter efficiency–no additional trainable parameters are introduced. Second, SimAM provides true 3D attention, simultaneously considering channel and spatial dimensions, which is natural for weighting the feature map _z_ that has both hidden units (channels) and token positions. Third, the energy-based formulation directly captures distinctive features, aligning with our goal of amplifying sparse personalized signals that deviate from common patterns. 

Compared to simply increasing the LoRA rank _r_ or deploying an independent learnable attention module, SimLoRA introduces negligible training computational overhead (with less than a 5% rise in total training time) and delivers interpretable, data-adaptive weighting. We further provide a theoretical complexity analysis to quantify the online inference overhead incurred by our dynamic attention map. For a standard Transformer layer with hidden dimension _d_ and sequence length _L_ , the forward computation is dominated by self-attention with complexity _O_ ( _L_<sup>2</sup> _· d_ ) and feed-forward networks with complexity _O_ ( _L · d_<sup>2</sup> ). The extra computation brought by the embedded SimAM module in SimLoRA–including channel-wise mean and variance calculation, followed by element-wise subtraction, division, and sigmoid activation applied to the intermediate feature **_z_** _∈_ R<sup>_d×L_</sup> –only yields a strict complexity of _O_ ( _L · d_ ). As _O_ ( _L · d_ ) is asymptotically subdominant to the quadratic and bilinear bottleneck terms of large-scale LLMs (we adopt Qwen-14B as the test model in all experiments), the incremental FLOPs per Transformer layer remain marginal. Although static weight merging is not supported in our framework, the extra inference latency is theoretically bounded and expected to be minimal in practice. The ablation results in Section 4 validate the necessity of SimAMbased weighting: substituting SimAM weights with random counterparts leads to clear performance degradation, while removing the SimAM branch degenerates our model to vanilla LoRA.

_Citizen Card QA Dataset (Base Dataset)_ . We collected 100,000 historical QA records from the citizen card platform. After data cleaning (removing duplicates and filtering noise), we obtained a dataset containing 70,000 QA pairs, split into 65,000 training samples and 5000 test samples. Each sample follows the following format: 

{ "instruction": "user query text", "output": "response text", "history": [] } 

Approximately 3% of the queries contain personalized information such as specific timeframes, individual social security contribution status, consumption records, or location details–information that reflects users’ specific circumstances and service needs. 

_Human Feedback Dataset (Reward Dataset)._ When users are dissatisfied with automated responses, they can escalate to human agents. The platform records both the system-generated response and the subsequent human response, with human responses typically exhibiting higher accuracy, better alignment with user needs, and more appropriate tone. { "instruction": "user query text", "output": ["human_response", "model_response"], "history": [] } 

The first response in the output list is the preferred (human) response; the second is the dispreferred (system) response. This preference pairing is used to train the reward model for RLHF.

#### **_4.1.3 Evaluation metrics_** 

We used multiple metrics to comprehensively evaluate model performance. 

_ROUGE_ (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) measures the overlap between generated responses and reference responses. Following standard practice, we report ROUGE-1 (unigram overlap), ROUGE-2 (bigram overlap), and ROUGE-L (longest common subsequence). ROUGE metrics capture content coverage and are widely used for text generation evaluation. 

_BLEU_ (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) measures the precision of n-grams with a brevity penalty. We report BLEU-1 (unigram precision), BLEU-2 (bigram precision), and BLEU-4 (4-gram precision). BLEU metrics capture response fluency and exact matching quality. 

_BERTScore_ evaluates semantic similarity between generated responses and reference answers using contextual embeddings from BERT-base-chinese. Unlike ROUGE and BLEU, BERTScore captures deeper semantic meaning beyond surface-level n-gram matching, providing a more robust measure of answer correctness. 

_Pairwise Accuracy_ measures the reward model’s ability to distinguish between preferred and dispreferred responses. For a given prompt and a pair of responses (human and model-generated), if the reward model assigns a higher score to the human response, the prediction is correct. This metric evaluates reward model quality.

#### **4.2 Results** 

#### **_4.2.1 Comparison of fine-tuning methods_** 

Table 2 reports the comparison between standard LoRA and the proposed SimLoRA on the citizen card QA test set. SimLoRA consistently outperforms LoRA across all metrics, with improvements ranging from 1.5 to 2.9 percentage points for ROUGE scores and 1.7 to 2.4 percentage points for BLEU scores. The differences are statistically significant (paired t-test, _p <_ 0 _._ 01). 

The stratified analysis presented in Table 3 provides strong evidence that SimLoRA’s performance improvements are concentrated in areas consistent with enhanced handling of personalized information. Three key observations support this conclusion: 

First, LoRA struggles significantly more with personalized queries than generic queries, as evidenced by the 3.1 percentage point gap between its ROUGE-L scores on these subsets (0.431 vs. 0.462). This gap reflects LoRA’s difficulty in capturing sparse, contextdependent features that appear infrequently in training data. Second, SimLoRA effectively bridges this gap, reducing the performance difference between personalized and generic queries from 3.1 to 0.6 percentage points. We note that SimLoRA’s personalizedquery score (0.482) remains marginally below its generic-query score (0.488), indicating the gap is narrowed but not fully closed–reflecting the intrinsic challenge of learning from sparse signals and suggesting room for further improvement. This narrowing of the gap demonstrates that SimLoRA’s attention-weighted updates successfully amplify sparse personalized signals. Third, the improvement ratios clearly show that SimLoRA’s gains are concentrated on personalized queries: the ROUGE-L improvement on personalized queries is 1.96 times that on generic queries, and the BERTScore improvement is 1.60 times that on generic queries. This disproportionate improvement strongly supports our core hypothesis that SimLoRA’s attention mechanism enhances the capture of rare but critical user-specific features.

_Contributions._ From a theoretical perspective, we introduced a novel integration of a neuroscience-inspired attention mechanism with parameter-efficient fine-tuning, demonstrating that feature-aware weighting of low-rank updates can effectively capture sparse signals in training data. Practically, we provided a computationally efficient method with minimal overhead (less than 5% increase in training time, no inference cost) that can be easily applied to existing LoRA-based pipelines. We also released two real-world datasets to facilitate future research in public service QA. 

_Limitations._ First, our experiments focus on a single domain (citizen card services) and a single base model (Qwen-14B-Chat); generalization to other domains and architectures requires further validation. Second, while SimLoRA improves learning from sparse personalized features, it does not address zero-shot personalization where such information is entirely absent from training data. Third, our RLHF implementation relies on preference data collected from user escalation logs, which introduces three distinct sources of bias: (i) selection bias–escalated cases overrepresent queries where the system failed, skewing toward harder scenarios; (ii) response style bias–human agents use customer-service phrasing that the reward model may learn to prefer independently of factual accuracy; and (iii) a compliance–helpfulness trade-off where human responses may favor leniency over strict policy adherence. While our reward model achieves 79.1% pairwise accuracy and 75.1% AI Labeler Alignment, these metrics measure consistency with held-out preference data that inherit the same biases, and therefore do not independently validate factual correctness. Addressing these biases through domain-expert annotation and style-controlled reward modeling is an important direction for future work. 

_Future Directions._ Several promising directions emerge from this work. First, crossdomain validation across diverse public service domains (e.g., healthcare, taxation, education) would establish the generalizability of SimLoRA beyond citizen card services. Second, the current attention weight analysis is illustrative rather than systematic; a rigorous statistical study quantifying the distribution of SimAM attention weights across the full personalized query subset–for instance, computing mean attention ratios for personalized versus generic tokens–would further strengthen mechanistic evidence for the attention-amplification hypothesis. Third, bias mitigation strategies for escalationlog-based RLHF warrant dedicated investigation, including: (i) re-annotation of a preference pair subset by public service domain experts to evaluate responses specifically on factual correctness and policy compliance, (ii) style-controlled reward modeling that disentangles content quality from customer-service stylistic features, and (iii) Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) or rejection sampling to bypass separate reward model training and reduce the propagation of style biases, as well as (iv) deployment-level A/B testing to measure actual user satisfaction, re-escalation rates, and human-agent workload as ecologically valid outcome metrics. Fourth, extending SimLoRA to multimodal scenarios (e.g., processing uploaded documents or images) would broaden applicability in citizen-facing services. Finally, long-term deployment studies that measure user satisfaction and operational efficiency would complement the offline evaluations presented here.
