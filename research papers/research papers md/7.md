#### **1. Introduction** 

The increasing complexity of cyber–physical systems demands intelligent decision-making frameworks that can operate autonomously, adapt dynamically, and continuously learn from their environments [1]. Two rapidly evolving paradigms, digital twins (DTs) [2] and agentic artificial intelligence (agentic AI) [3], are converging to address this challenge. DTs provide dynamic, data-driven virtual replicas of physical systems that enable real-time monitoring, prediction, and optimization [4]. Agentic AI refers to artificial intelligence that autonomously perceives system states, identifies operational goals, formulates and evaluates decision strategies, executes actions through adaptive control mechanisms, and continuously refines its performance based on feedback and changing conditions. The integration of these two technologies creates new opportunities for intelligent, self-evolving decision systems capable of optimizing complex operations in real-world environments [5,6]. 

Despite rapid advances in both fields, much of the literature still treats DTs as passive mirrors of assets and AI agents as isolated reasoning modules [7–9]. For example, in the grid-balancing setting of Fig. 1, intermittent sources (wind, solar) feed both the grid and utility-scale 

storage, while baseload plants (hydro, nuclear, thermal) anchor supply. On the demand side, heterogeneous loads (homes and commercial buildings, EV fast-charging, energy-intensive industry, and retail) pull power through the transmission network. Storage and power-to-X assets (batteries, thermal stores, electrolyzers producing hydrogen/synthetic fuels) are the pivotal buffers, absorbing surplus renewables, shifting energy from milliseconds to days, and returning it as electricity, heat, or fuels. Yet when control of these assets is siloed, situational awareness fractures across the supply-storage-demand chain, blocking online adaptation, cross-domain learning (physics ↔ markets ↔ weather ↔ behavior), and true closed-loop control. Today’s operations still lean on predefined rules and periodically solved centralized optimizations that are brittle under non-stationarity (volatile demand, renewable intermittency, communication delays, model drift, unforeseen contingencies) [10,11], and the absence of a unified cognitive substrate limits explainability and hampers collaborative decisions among operators, markets, and automated controllers [12,13].

Recent studies have extended DT applications to cyber–physical and infrastructure systems, including smart manufacturing [18], transportation networks [19], and energy systems [20]. In the context of the electrical power grid, DTs have been explored for state estimation, contingency analysis, and fault prediction [21]. However, most existing grid DTs rely on deterministic optimization or rule-based control, which limits their ability to adapt to changing demand, renewable energy variability, and distributed generation [22]. Traditional DTs are excellent at representation but limited in reasoning, since they simulate but rarely decide. This shortcoming shows the need for cognitive extensions that allow DTs to autonomously learn and act within evolving operational contexts. 

#### _2.2. Agentic artificial intelligence_ 

The recent rise of agentic AI marks a major shift from task-specific automation to goal-oriented autonomy [23]. Built upon LLMs and multimodal learning, agentic AI architectures employ iterative reasoning, tool use, and environment interaction to pursue high-level objectives with minimal human intervention. These systems embody an ‘‘agentic loop’’ of perception, reasoning, action, and reflection, enabling selfimprovement through experience. Research on multi-agent systems (MAS) and cognitive architectures such as SOAR [24], Adaptive Control of Thought-Rational (ACT-R) [25], and more recently, LLM-based frameworks like AutoGPT [26], have contributed to the theoretical foundation for agentic AI. These developments emphasize modularity, collaboration, and adaptive learning. However, most implementations operate in digital-only environments, where agents interact within simulated or data-defined contexts, lacking a connection to live, physical systems [27]. As a result, agentic AI systems often face challenges in grounding their reasoning in real-world dynamics, data fidelity, and safety-critical constraints, areas where DTs can play a vital role. 

#### _2.3. Integration of AI and digital twin models_ 

Integrating AI with DTs has been explored primarily through machine learning-enhanced twins, where predictive models or reinforcement learning agents optimize DT behavior [28]. Such hybrid approaches have shown promise in domains like predictive maintenance, smart manufacturing, and energy management. For example, reinforcement learning-based DTs have been used for optimal control of microgrids and dynamic load balancing [29]. However, these systems typically embed narrow AI models within DT frameworks rather than integrating agentic AI architectures capable of autonomous goal pursuit, collaboration, and self-reflection. Emerging work suggests that coupling LLM-driven reasoning agents with DTs can enable adaptive control and explainable decision-making [30]. Still, there remains a gap in defining systematic architectures that formalize how agentic AI components interact with DT representations in a closed feedback loop. Moreover, the majority of AI-DT integration efforts in power systems focus on predictive accuracy or optimization efficiency, with limited attention to cognitive adaptability, inter-agent communication, or real-time learning. 

#### _2.4. Research gap and motivation_ 

While prior studies have advanced DT fidelity and AI autonomy independently, few have achieved a unified framework that integrates both into a cohesive, intelligent decision-making system. Current DTbased control architectures lack adaptive reasoning, whereas current agentic AI systems lack physical grounding and real-time synchronization [31]. For electrical grid management, this disconnect restricts the potential for continuous power balancing, distributed coordination, and resilience under uncertainty [32]. 

The insights drawn from the literature highlight persistent limitations in existing AI-DT integrations, particularly the lack of mechanisms 

for continuous learning, coordinated reasoning across agents, and tightly coupled cognitive-simulation loops. These gaps indicate the need for an architectural framework that systematically operationalizes agentic intelligence while leveraging the DT as a unified, realtime representation of system state. Motivated by these observations, the next section introduces a multilayer integration framework designed to address these shortcomings and support adaptive, goal-driven decision-making in complex environments. 

#### **3. Integrated conceptual and system architecture** 

This section presents a unified multilayer integration framework that combines agentic AI with DT to enable adaptive, goal-oriented, and physically grounded decision-making. The approach establishes a closed cognitive-cyber–physical loop connecting perception, reasoning, action, and feedback. We demonstrate the framework through power balancing in electrical grid management, a domain requiring realtime adaptability and coordination among distributed resources. Fig. 2 illustrates the high-level architecture. 

#### _3.1. Core concept_ 

At the center of the framework is a bidirectional interaction between agentic AI systems and the DT environment. The DT is a living model of the physical grid, continuously synchronized with sensor, meter, and control data. Agentic AI agents operate as cognitive entities that interpret this model, reason about system states, and select actions aligned with operational objectives. The DT thus evolves from a passive monitor to an active cognitive ecosystem, a shared reasoning substrate where agents simulate, test, and coordinate actions before implementation. The overall system forms a continuous perceptionreasoning-action–feedback loop in which decisions adapt in real time to changing conditions. 

#### _3.2. Multilayer integration framework_ 

The architecture comprises multiple autonomous AI agents interacting with a shared DT, with a feedback and adaptation mechanism driving continuous improvement. Each agent has perception, reasoning, and execution capabilities, while the DT provides coordination, synchronization, and predictive simulation. The framework is organized into six interdependent layers: 

_(a) Multimodal perception layer._ Interfaces the physical system, DT, and agents by aggregating heterogeneous data streams (sensors, weather, markets, and logs) into semantically consistent inputs. For power balancing it collects generation/consumption data, voltage, current, frequency indicators, renewable forecasts, and market or policy constraints. 

_(b) Knowledge and data layer._ Serves as the cognitive memory combining structured databases, ontologies, and historical records with domain knowledge. It provides shared access to load profiles, generation forecasts, network topology, component health, and regulatory constraints for both agents and the DT. 

_(c) Reasoning and learning layer (LLM-based)._ The cognitive core of each agent. LLMs with domain-specific learning enable context-aware interpretation, anomaly detection, prediction of system behavior, hypothesis generation, and cross-agent coordination. 

_(d) Decision-making layer (LLM-based)._ Translates insights into actionable strategies by evaluating alternatives under multi-objective criteria (stability, efficiency, sustainability). In grid operations this includes dispatching storage, curtailing renewables, reconfiguring topology, or triggering demand response. Candidate actions are validated in the DT before real-world execution.

#### **6. Future research directions** 

While the proposed framework demonstrates the feasibility and benefits of integrating agentic AI and DT technologies for intelligent grid balancing, several avenues merit further exploration. First, scalable multi-agent coordination and distributed optimization is needed to move from small prototypes to regional and national grids. Promising directions include consensus-based and hierarchical optimization, federated learning for model sharing without raw data exchange, and communication protocols that adapt bandwidth use to operational urgency while preserving global feasibility. Second, future work should incorporate uncertainty-aware decision making. Renewable availability, load behavior, and contingencies are inherently stochastic; therefore, probabilistic forecasting, ensemble models, and scenario-based or chance-constrained optimization are natural extensions. Such methods would allow agents to quantify and propagate uncertainty through the DT, producing risk-aware dispatch plans with explicit reliability margins. Third, tighter integration with energy markets and policy layers can align operational objectives with economic incentives and sustainability goals. Agentic AI could autonomously generate bids, negotiate demand response, and evaluate policy impacts within the twin before execution. Embedding market rules and regulatory constraints into the shared knowledge base would enable closed-loop simulations that co-optimize cost, emissions, and reliability. Fourth, advancing toward cognitive DTs and human-in-the-loop interaction will be essential for trust and accountability. Research is needed on explainable LLM-based reasoning, interactive natural-language interfaces for operators, and mechanisms for human oversight that can intercede, audit, or revise autonomous actions without breaking real-time guarantees. Fifth, the architecture should be generalized across domains. Many large-scale cyber–physical systems, such as water distribution, transportation logistics, and manufacturing, exhibit similar coupling of prediction, optimization, and control. Developing reusable ontologies, model templates, and agent roles would support transferability while respecting domain-specific constraints. Finally, ethics, security, and regulation demand sustained attention. Future work should address verifiable and auditable AI pipelines, encrypted and authenticated inter-agent communication, adversarial robustness, privacy preservation, and alignment with evolving standards. Co-design of technical and regulatory frameworks will be critical for responsible deployment of autonomous decision systems at scale.

By enabling cognitive, self-adaptive, and explainable control, the integration of agentic AI and DTs represents a fundamental step toward the next generation of autonomous, resilient, and human-aligned intelligent infrastructures. 

Future research may explore scaling this architecture to multidomain environments, such as transportation, manufacturing, and smart buildings, where multiple DTs must interoperate and coordinate across shared resources. Additional directions include developing standardized protocols for agent, which include DT interaction, investigating mechanisms for cross-domain cognitive load distribution, and evaluating performance in real-world deployments. These avenues will further advance the practicality, robustness, and generalizability of agentic AI-DT systems.

#### **7. Conclusion and future works** 

This paper proposed an integrated agentic AI-DT architecture for intelligent decision-making. The framework unifies perception, reasoning, decision-making, and feedback into a continuous cognitive-cyber– physical loop, enabling autonomous and adaptive control. Within this architecture, multimodal perception provides real-time situational awareness, while LLM-based reasoning and learning layers generate context-aware predictions and strategies. The decision-making layer formulates and solves cost-minimization problems subject to grid constraints, and the feedback layer enables continuous adaptation through real-time data and outcome evaluation. The simulation results demonstrated how the proposed architecture can forecast load, optimize generation dispatch, coordinate power flows, and reduce operational cost while maintaining grid stability.
