This is a representative scenario of complex knowledge work, which goes beyond merely processing information and instead requires the creation, distribution, and application of (expert) knowledge. Skilled individuals autonomously manage information and data to navigate complex situations and produce tailored outcomes (Drucker, 1999; Pyöriä, 2005; Schultze, 2000). This type of knowledge work requires a deep understanding of framed experience, values, contextual information, and expert insight. Accordingly, the tasks involved are typically unstructured, non-routine, and intricately tailored to specific situations (Davenport et al., 1996; Davenport & Prusak, 1998; Heerwagen et al., 2004; Janz et al., 1997). 

With the advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), automation in information systems (IS) is entering a new phase. While earlier technical automation approaches such as robotic process automation (RPA) focused on deterministic, rule-based execution of routine tasks (Hofmann et al., 2020; Jimenez-Ramirez et al., 2019), recent developments increasingly extend into unstructured, contextsensitive domains (Acharya et al., 2025). The growing accessibility of AI-as-a-Service (AIaaS) (Lins et al., 2021) andlow-codedevelopmentplatforms(BockandFrank, 2021) lowerstechnicalbarriers,whilegenerativeAImodelsprovide adaptive reasoning and language capabilities that were previously out of reach (Fetzer et al., 2025; Feuerriegel et al., 2024; Schneider et al., 2024). 

In light of this convergence of increasingly capable AI models, accessible AI infrastructure, and lowered development barriers, we witness the emergence of a new phenomenon: **Multi-agent AI (MAAI)** . Unlike traditional automation pipelines, MAAI consists of ensembles of AIbased agents (autonomous entities capable of perception, decision-making, and action) whose interactions are not governed by predetermined workflows but emerge dynamically in response to context. Building on core concepts from agentic IS, such as agency, delegation, and governance, MAAI extends these ideas to multi-agent settings in which multiple AI-based agents must coordinate, delegate, and adapt their behavior collectively to address complex, unstructured tasks (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Zhou et al., 2024). This development does not emerge in a conceptual vacuum. Instead, MAAI revives long-standing principles of multi-agent systems (MAS) research in computer science (CS) (Ferber & Weiss, 1999; Wooldridge, 2009), which emphasized the intertwined challenges of agent and society design (da Silva & Demazeau, 2002; Demazeau, 1997), and integrates them with IS discourses on process automation (Engel et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2025), agentic IS (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Feuerriegel et al., 2024), and AI-enabled cognitive capabilities<sup>1</sup> .

# **Conceptual roots** 

The conceptual foundations of MAAI originate in the longstanding tradition of MAS research in CS. MAS emerged as a response to problems that exceed the capabilities of monolithic systems, highlighting that many computational tasks require distributed problem solving, coordination, and collective intelligence (Ferber & Weiss, 1999; Wooldridge, 2009). Classic MAS theory identifies two inseparable design challenges: the _agent design_ problem (how to construct autonomous entities capable of perception, reasoning, and action) and the _society design_ problem (how to organize multiple agents into effective collectives that can pursue complex objectives in shared environments) (Wooldridge, 2009). These represent the micro- and macro-level perspectives of MAS, yet they are not orthogonal: individual design choices shape emergent group behaviors, while organizational structures impose constraints on agent reasoning and interaction. To systematize this interdependence, MAS research advanced integrative frameworks such as the vowel paradigm (da Silva & Demazeau, 2002; Demazeau, 1997), which characterizes MAS across five complementary views: agent (individual capabilities and decision logic), interaction (communication and coordination protocols), environment (the structuring role of shared contexts), and organization (roles, institutions, and coordination mechanisms). 

Building on this foundation, the following subsections trace how these classical ideas resurface in MAAI. First, we distinguish MAAI from traditional MAS, clarifying analytical granularity and definitional ambiguities. Second, we outline the characteristics of AI-based agents in MAAI, emphasizing the perception–decision–action(–learn) cycle and the role of FMs. Third, we turn to their organization, situating MAAI at the intersection of process automation, agentic IS, and AI. Finally, we synthesize these roots into a layered conceptual framework that extends the MAS tradition into contemporary IS contexts. 

## **From traditional multi-agent systems to multi-agent AI** 

The interdependence of agent and society design complicates the classification of complex AI-based IS (Kuehl et al., 2022). A single software entity may be internally composed of multiple sub-agents, each meeting autonomy criteria, thereby blurring the distinction between single- and multiagent systems. Franklin and Graesser (1996) illustrate this 

with Sumpy, an agent whose layered architecture qualifies it simultaneously as an individual agent and as an MAS. Thus, whether an orchestrated workflow is analyzed as one composite agent or as an MAS depends on the chosen analytical granularity. Within the IS domain, however, such distinctions are less clear-cut, and boundaries between complex AI-based IS and MAS remain contested (Collins et al., 2021; Dorri et al., 2018; Hofmann et al., 2024; Sapkota et al., 2026). 

## **AI-based agents in multi-agent AI** 

AI-based agents in MAAI are grounded in established theories of intelligent and autonomous agents. Russell et al. (1995) describe an _intelligent agent_ as an entity that perceives its environment through sensors (perception) and acts (action) upon it through actuators to achieve goals, highlighting the perception–action loop as a fundamental cycle. Franklin and Graesser (1996) refine this to define an _autonomous agent_ as a system embedded in an environment that continuously senses (perception), acts (action), and adapts over time (decision/learn). Wooldridge and Jennings (1995) characterize agents by autonomy (decision), reactivity (perception), proactiveness (decision/action), and social ability (action/coordination), while Ferber and Weiss (1999) frame them as physical or virtual entities that perceive (perception), act (action), communicate (action/coordination), and pursue goals (decision) within evolving environments. Wooldridge (2009, p.15) synthesizes these perspectives as “a computer system that is situated in some environment, and that is capable of autonomous action in this environment in order to meet its design objectives” (perception–decision–action cycle). 

Building on this lineage, AI-based agents in MAAI operationalize the classical perception–decision–action(–learn) cycle but are both enhanced and constrained by the cognitive capabilities (Vernon et al., 2007) of AI-enabled IS (Kuehl et al., 2022). In practice, these capabilities are frequently provided through FMs (Bommasani et al., 2021; Schneider et al., 2024), which are often employed “as is” without further fine-tuning for specific tasks—contrasting with traditional reinforcementlearning(RL)agentsthatiterativelyadapttheir policies to the environment. Crucially, as Hofmann et al. (2024) cautions, autonomy should not be conceived as a binary property. Instead, it spans a spectrum from automated to semi-autonomous to fully autonomous systems, depending on their self-sufficiency, constraints in fulfilling goals, and networking capacities. This spectrum is essential for positioning MAAI: AI-based agents inherit MAS principles while emphasizing the contemporary flexibility, adaptivity, and coordination enabled by FMs (Bommasani et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025; Schneider et al., 2024).

## **Organization of AI-based agents** 

To address definitional uncertainties, we situate MAAI at the intersection of three foundational IS research streams— _process automation_ , _agentic IS_ , and _AI_ —which are deeply interdependent in digital market ecosystems. Their convergencecanbemeaningfullyinterpretedthroughtheinteraction-, environment-, and organization-oriented views of the vowel paradigm (da Silva & Demazeau, 2002; Demazeau, 1997). 

The first foundational stream, **process automation** , contributes to the environment-oriented view by providing the structural substrate: it traditionally involves transferring work that is organized into processes consisting of events, tasks, and decision points from humans to technology (Benbya et al., 2020; Engel et al., 2022), often to enhance productivity and reduce manual workload (Autor et al., 2003; Hofmann et al., 2020). RPA exemplifies this, using predefined business rules to automate routine tasks (Hofmann et al., 2020; IEEE Guide for Terms and Concepts in Intelligent Process Automation, 2017; Jimenez-Ramirez et al., 2019). It is especially effective in repetitive tasks (Haase et al., 2024; Willcocks et al., 2017). Yet, traditional IS theory has tended to frame such technologies as passive artifacts under human control (Baird and Maruping, 2021). This framing is increasingly challenged as process automation is augmented with AI capabilities, expanding its scope from deterministic rule-following toward cognitive automation that integrates probabilistic decision-making, adaptive classification, and context-sensitive responses (Engel et al., 2022; Hofmann et al., 2020; Janiesch et al., 2021). When embeddedwithinMAAI,processautomationthusshiftsfrom predictable orchestration in bounded environments toward dynamic and reconfigurable workflows, where agents can flexibly adapt to novel situations, negotiate interdependencies, and coordinate across heterogeneous socio-technical contexts (Liu et al., 2025). 

The second foundational stream, **agentic IS** , contributes to the interaction-oriented view by foregrounding delegation, coordination, and negotiation among heterogeneous agents, which are either human or computational (Holldack et al., 2026). Reflecting on agentic IS, Baird and Maruping (2021) developed the IS delegation framework. This framework defines agentic IS through three constructs: autonomous agents, their relational mechanisms (e.g., delegation), and the tasks they collaboratively pursue. At the core of agentic IS are agents, human or computational, which are autonomous entities capable of perceiving and acting within an environment, making independent decisions, and interacting with each other (Russell et al., 1995). In doing so, they cooperate and occasionally conflict to achieve individual or collective goals (Baird and Maruping, 2021). Agentic IS artifacts challenge traditional IS views of passive technologies by autonomously 

initiating actions, managing tasks, and assuming roles and responsibilities (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Feuerriegel et al., 2024). In this context, Baird and Maruping (2021) propose rethinking IS theory to incorporate AI artifacts as agentic actors. Similarly, Schuetz and Venkatesh (2020) argue that cognitive, agentic systems exceed the explanatory scope of existing models of user-information technology interaction, as they can adapt, perceive their environment, and interact with humans and technologies. While Baird and Maruping (2021) primarily focus on dyads of human and agentic IS artifacts, configurations involving multiple interacting artifacts align with MAS (Dorri et al., 2018). However, definitional ambiguity persists. Some scholars treat agentic systems broadly, encompassing single- and multi-agent setups (Baird and Maruping, 2021), while others reserve “multi-agent” for systems with multiple AI agents operating in collaboration (Hughes et al., 2025). This inconsistency is exacerbated by competing labels: Baird and Maruping (2021) use the term “agentic IS artifacts,” while Schuetz and Venkatesh (2020) prefer “cognitive computing systems.” Other works refer to “AI agents” or “MAS” (Göldi & Rietsche, 2024; Hughes et al., 2025), or to more specific labels such as “large language model (LLM)-based” (Xi et al., 2025), contributing to conceptual ambiguity, communication challenges, and fragmentation across IS discourse. 

For MAAI, agentic IS offers essential design principles: delegation, autonomy, and collaboration. These principles articulate how complex tasks can be distributed among multiple autonomous entities and coordinated through relational mechanisms, aligning naturally with the interaction-oriented dimension of the vowel paradigm. They also foreground the need for governance structures—protocols for coordination, conflict resolution, and oversight—when autonomy is distributed across many agents. While the IS delegation framework (Baird and Maruping, 2021) provides a pivotal foundation by centering on the human-agent dyad and its delegation mechanisms, our MAAI framework extends this perspective to the orchestration of multiple AI-based agents, moving beyond dyadic delegation toward multi-agent coordination in complex socio-technical environments. 

The third foundational stream, **AI** , expands both the organization- and environment-oriented views by furnishing agents with cognitive capabilities for perception, reasoning, and adaptive behavior (Berente et al., 2021; Kuehl et al., 2022). In the environment-oriented view, AI enables agents to interpret unstructured inputs, learn from data, and adapt actions to dynamic contexts, thereby extending process automation beyond deterministic routines into probabilistic and context-sensitive domains (cognitive automation) (Engel etal., 2022;Hofmannetal., 2020;Janieschetal., 2021).Inthe organization-oriented view, advances in FMs have enlarged the feasible scope of agent roles: agents can not only execute

## **The nature of multi-agent AI** 

Bringing these streams together, MAAI is best understood as an architectural paradigm rather than a singular technology. It represents a resurgence and synthesis of long-standing ideas from MAS, AI, and IS research concepts. What distinguishes the current moment is the maturity of AI techniques, especially FMs. These advances make it far more feasible to construct ensembles of AI-based agents that perceive, reason, act, (and learn) collaboratively, moving beyond the rigid, rule-based scripts of earlier automation toward dynamic, reconfigurable, and knowledge-intensive workflows. 

Our choice of the term “Multi-agent AI” is deliberate. While “agentic AI” has gained traction in industry discourse, it risks overstating novelty and obscuring continuity with MAS research. The phrase also underemphasizes the plurality of agents, which is central to the phenomenon. By contrast, MAAI explicitly anchors these developments in the MAS tradition, acknowledging that many so-called “agentic” innovations are rediscoveries of long-established principles (Sapkota et al., 2026) (cf. Table 1 in the Appendix). Framing the phenomenon as MAAI ensures that new AIbased agent collectives are understood as building upon—not reinventing—the conceptual and technical scaffolding of MAS. 

From the convergence of process automation (structure), agentic IS (interaction), and AI (cognition) and drawing explicitly on the agent-, interaction-, environment-, and organization-oriented views of the vowel paradigm (da Silva & Demazeau, 2002; Demazeau, 1997), we derive five interdependent layered components (abbreviated as C1 to C5) that characterize MAAI. Figure 1 visualizes these components in 

an “onion-like” hierarchy. Each layer represents a debatespace cluster formed at the intersection of IS research streams and vowel paradigm views defining MAS. The _FM layer (C1)_ corresponds to the _agent-oriented_ view by defining the cognitive and representational capabilities of AI-based agents. The _data-centric perception and action layer (C2)_ links to the _environment-oriented_ view by specifying how agents sense and act upon digital environments. _Dynamic orchestration (C3)_ reflects the _interaction-oriented_ view through mechanisms of coordination, delegation, and emergent task structuring among agents. The _agent-integrated workflow layer (C4)_ aligns with the _organization-oriented_ view by embedding agent activity into institutional processes, governance structures, and routinized work practices. Finally, the _interaction interface (C5)_ connects these layers back to human actors, enabling meaningful oversight, collaboration, and accountability. Together, these components extend the MAS tradition of agent and society design into IS contexts, embedding micro-level autonomy within macro-level structures of coordination, governance, and human interaction. ## **Component C2: Data-centric perception and action** 

Building upon FMs, AI-based agents comprise perception and action, composing C2 to interact with their environment (Kuehl et al., 2022; Xi et al., 2025). In line with prior research in CS, we define agents as entities that are modeled using concepts traditionally associated with human characteristics, such as autonomy, social ability, reactivity, and proactiveness (Goodwin, 1995; Wooldridge & Jennings, 1995). Rooted in MAS and informed by agentic IS concepts, AI-based agents extend traditional process automation by incorporating human-centric capabilities like collaboration, decision-making, and adaptability. Consequently, each AIbased agent must incorporate mechanisms to perceive and act upon its environment, grounded in a data-centric approach (Jakubiketal., 2024).Thisapproachacknowledgesthatwhile the underlying FM—its architecture and capacities—remain fixed, its effective adaptation and specialization are based on information in the form of data it receives and processes. 

In data-centric AI, the primary idea for improving system performance and tailoring behaviors lies not in modifying the AI models’ architecture but rather in refining, selecting, and structuring the data that AI-based agents perceive and use as context (Bhatt et al., 2024). Consequently, the perception component must sense and prepare diverse forms of data, optimizing them for the FM’s internal representations. Although the FM’s fundamental capabilities remain constant, the agent’s skillfulness, domain relevance, and ability to handle evolving tasks are shaped by the data that it ingests and transforms into actionable insights. Since FMs store their learned representations in architectural parameters (weights) obtained from extensive prior training, they only influence their performance by altering the data they process, both as input and through the retrieval of external information. Perception in MAAI operates entirely on data: it transforms raw environmental inputs into structured, coherent formats that align with the FM’s learned latent space of representations. This includes external data from sensors, documents, user inputs, application programming interfaces (APIs), and other sources, as well as internally managed context (e.g., previously generated text or prompt-engineered instructions). Through techniques like retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) (Klesel & Wittmann, 2025; Lewis et al., 

2020; Li et al., 2025), active feature acquisition (Hübotter et al., 2024), structured templates (Wei et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2025), and APIs (Liang et al., 2024), the perception component curates and enhances the data delivered to the FM, effectively guiding the model’s output without changing its core parameters. AI-based agents can access external databases or knowledge repositories through these mechanisms, retrieving relevant information dynamically to augment the context available to the FM. Here, the choice and quality of data—which documents to retrieve, what contexts to include, and how to structure the information—enforce distinct characteristics of the AI-based agents. 

Once the FM processes the perceived data and produces an output, the action component interprets this output and executes actions in a data-mediated environment. Actions themselves are represented and performed through data: producing a textual response, generating a visual artifact, calling an API, or interacting with external systems are all rendered as data flows. This concept often involves leveraging specialized tools that enable the FM to perform specific functions, such as engaging in vocal conversations. By conceptualizing perception and action as data-centric processes—where even the agent’s “touch” on its environment occurs through data-level transformations and interfaces—agents operate within and manipulate data representations. In addition to perception and action, C2 contains the capability to store and manage data, thereby increasing the contextual richness available to the FM. This stored context provides continuity across tasks, enabling AI-based agents to refine their decisions and responses based on historical and situational data. 

Consider again the workflow of an insurance company handling a customer’s car damage claim (Fig. 2). Each specialized AI-based agent—be it the policy analyst, claims processor, or customer service agent—derives its expertise and context from both static and dynamic sources. Static elements such as predefined roles, agent profiles, and base prompts encode task-specific behavior and domain knowledge. Dynamically retrieved data, such as policy documents, customer records, or regulatory APIs, are then integrated during task execution to contextualize perception and guide action. For instance, the policy analyst can interpret insurance clauses by retrieving relevant documents, while the customer service agent adjusts tone and content by drawing on conversation history, customer records, and domainspecific prompts. After processing this curated input data, the FM’s output directs the subsequent actions, enabling context-specific responses such as generating reports, sending messages, or interacting with external tools. These actions, too, remain data-driven: both perception and execution are shaped by what data can be accessed, structured, and operationalized in the agent’s environment.

## **Component C3: Dynamic orchestration** 

To effectively integrate the outputs and actions of various agents and maintain coherence within the agent-integrated workflow, AI-based agents must possess a distinct identity that grants them social standing (Bisk et al., 2020). This identity is essential for enabling social cooperation and competition within an MAS. The implementation of such a system comprises a registry of role-specific agents, each designedtobeselectedanddeployedbasedonthedemandsof the task at hand (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). This composition must be dynamically orchestrated in alignment with the specific requirements of the workflow. The orchestration should be sufficiently adaptable, allowing each workflow to autonomously determine its optimal configuration of agents. This ensures the orchestration can address the full complexity inherent in knowledge-intensive tasks. The orchestrated agents’ capabilities are extended by connecting external services such as messaging applications or payment tools. In line with previous architectures like service-oriented computing, this integration allows orchestrated agents to execute actions that require interaction with external systems, broadening the scope of tasks they can handle (Calisti et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2025; Tapia et al., 2010). 

In the context of MAS, the division of labor principle underpins the specialization of agents, where each agent, equipped with domain-specific expertise, is optimized for executing particular tasks. This specialization enhances the efficiency and precision of task execution, as it minimizes the overhead associated with task switching by decomposing complex workflows into discrete, manageable subtasks. Xi et al. (2025) delineate agent interactions into two primary paradigms: cooperative interaction for complementarity and adversarial interaction for strategic advancement. Cooperative interaction involves agents that continuously evaluate their counterparts’ operational states and capabilities, engaging in collaborative task execution and knowledge exchange (Li et al., 2023). This interaction can be either disordered or ordered. In disordered cooperation, multiple agents operate with a high degree of autonomy, freely contributing their inputs and feedback within the system, which may result in misaligned objectives and potential disruptions in workflow orchestration (Mandi et al., 2024; Xi et al., 2025). Conversely, ordered cooperation adheres to predefined communication protocols, where agents sequentially process information, with downstream agents dependent on the outputs of upstream counterparts (Li et al., 2023, 2024). Dynamic orchestration mechanisms, such as a moderator agent, can be deployed to manage such interactions effectively to regulate communication flows and ensure adherence to structured review processes. In adversarial settings, agents dynamically reconfigure their strategies in response to realtime environmental feedback and the actions of competing 

agents (Abdelnabi et al., 2024). This adaptive behavior is crucial in adversarial MAS, where the objective is to optimize decision-making and enhance the quality of responses in high-stakes scenarios (Xi et al., 2025). AI-based agents may independently pursue optimization objectives, such as utility maximization, which aligns with principles of game theory (Mao et al., 2023). However, to maintain a structured competitive environment, deploying a moderator agent can facilitate the orchestration of interactions, ensuring that the competitive dynamics remain aligned with the overall system objectives. 

In our insurance company use case, dynamic orchestration is essential for managing cooperative agent interactions. Cooperative interactions allow agents to independently contribute towards aligning on a common objective, while enabling the system to flexibly adapt the workflow to the situation at hand. In a simple coverage inquiry, the moderator agent orchestrates a minimal workflow: the policy analyst retrieves and interprets the relevant policy clauses, the claims processor cross-checks the submitted information against historical cases, and the customer service agent drafts the final explanation. Once the required information is aligned, the moderator terminates the workflow. By contrast, a highercomplexity case with potential fraud indicators triggers a markedly different, dynamically assembled workflow. Here, the customer service agent analyzes anomalies in the submitted images, inconsistencies in timestamps, or mismatches with prior claims. This agent may engage in multiple iterative loops: requesting further evidence from specialized fraud detection agents, re-evaluating policy constraints, or escalating the case to a human fraud investigator. Throughout this process, the moderator adapts the orchestration strategy in real time, deciding which agents to involve, how to sequence interactions, and when to switch from cooperative to more adversarial evaluation patterns. Across both scenarios, the moderator agent regulates the information flow, resolves conflicts between partial outputs, and aligns the contributions of all involved agents.

Given the inherent flexibility and complexity of many realworld tasks, they necessitate an agent-integrated workflow and dynamic decision-making process, which may be automatically created within MAAI (Liu et al., 2025). Rooted in the “information pull” paradigm, this component is conceptualized as a dynamic workflow, generated in direct response to emergent information needs (Cichocki et al., 1998). In the context of MAAI, this dynamic workflow is operationalized through the orchestration of AI-based agents, which execute tasks guided by abstract design patterns. Van der Aalst et al. ## **Component C5: Interaction interface** 

The interaction interface serves as the entry point for both human and system-level engagement with MAAI. It encompasses not only user interfaces (UIs) but also APIs, enabling integration with external systems and organizational platforms. While the previous components enable AI-based agent coordination and agent-integrated workflow generation, the interaction interface ensures that these processes are accessible, traceable, and actionable. As such, the UI constitutes the critical socio-technical junction where the system becomes actionable in organizational settings. Effective UIs are also essential for enabling human oversight and intervention, especially in complex, automated workflows (Sterz et al., 2024). Drawing on IS perspectives (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Schuetz & Venkatesh, 2020), this component plays a dual role: supporting human-AI collaboration through explainable, adaptive, and role-specific interfaces, andfacilitatingmachine-to-machinecommunicationthrough structured endpoints. In organizational settings, such interfaces must balance transparency with usability—offering 

dashboards, conversational agents, or monitoring tools for internal users, and streamlined interaction channels for external systems. This goes beyond the necessity of having any interaction interface—it raises design requirements related to explainability, adaptive feedback, and trust-building mechanisms in human-AI collaboration. Designing an appropriate interaction interface for MAAI thus depends on the system’s purpose and user roles. For end-users, chatbot interfaces or decision dashboards may suffice to deliver responses and gather input. For internal users (e.g., employees monitoring agent-integrated workflows), the UI must support traceability of agent interactions, intervention capabilities, and contextual awareness. In both cases, prior work in IS suggests the importance of adaptive, stateful interfaces and role-specific views to manage complexity and promote trust (Gimpel et al., 2020; Gnewuch et al., 2017). 

In the insurance company use case, the interaction interface connects customers, employees, and the MAAI. A chatbot UI allows customers to submit claims and receive updates, while internal APIs dynamically feed outputs from policy analysts or claims processors into case management systems. These interaction interfaces ensure that the system remains comprehensible, responsive, and compatible with existing workflows. 

# **Challenges and emerging pathways for the IS community in electronic markets** 

The advent of modular agent-based AI systems—operating within organizations and across digital platforms, multisided marketplaces and networked business environments— marks a shift from conventional, narrowly defined automation toward platform-embedded agentic architectures that contribute to value creation, exchange mechanisms and business-ecosystem governance. Early research in electronic markets explored agent-based coordination, for example, autonomous buyers, sellers, and intermediaries negotiating prices (Kehagias et al., 2005; Oliver, 1996) or recommending products (Benbasat & Wang, 2005; Xiao & Benbasat, 2007). However, only recently have FM-enabled MAS entered this domain, bringing qualitatively new capabilities. In particular, FMs (Schneider et al., 2024), especially those employed in generative AI (Feuerriegel et al., 2024), represent transformative potential not only for automating knowledge work but for reconfiguring how digital marketplaces and service platforms mediate exchanges among actors. 

MAAI can ingest and reason over unstructured and nonstandardized data and contextualize it through multiple distributed agents offering different perspectives. This substantially expands what agent-based electronic markets can achieve, especially given the interdependencies and organizational boundaries that characterize modern platforms

and supply networks. In e-commerce and digital platforms, MAAI enables autonomous agents to negotiate, recommend, or coordinate logistics using not only structured catalog data but also customer reviews, images, sensor feeds, or socialmedia content. Across supply chains, interacting agents can support adaptive coordination optimizing procurement, forecasting demand, or scheduling shipments by integrating heterogeneous data sources (Nambisan, 2000; Singh et al., 2005; Wu et al., 2000). Therefore, MAAI represents a next stage in electronic market design: coordination and value exchange increasingly occur among heterogeneous, semiautonomous agents capable of rich contextual reasoning, rather than solely among human actors operating through rigid, structured data flows. 

For the IS community, and specifically within the domain of networked business and electronic markets, this development demands theoretical reflection and actionable guidance on how MAAI interplays with platform governance, marketplace coordination, business model innovation, and socio-economic integration. The conceptual framework presented in this work seeks to provide such guidance in the context of digital platforms and electronic markets. Each component addresses a core architectural layer of MAAI systems in platform settings, offering a scaffold for identifying

Practitioners— especially platform owners, digital marketplace operators, and ecosystem designers—can use it as a reference for designing modular, agent-based systems that enable value exchange, orchestration, and marketplace governance in realworld digital ecosystems. Some research questions naturally link to adjacent fields: those on C1–C2 overlap with work on LLMs, RAG and AI-based services in platforms; those on C4–C5 connect to issues of trust, transparency, humanagent interaction, and governance in electronic markets. By contrast, challenges around C3 (dynamic orchestration) are particularly distinctive for agent ecosystems within platformed or networked business contexts.

## **_Structure_ organizational and economic integration (Components C3–C4)** 

From a managerial and platform-ecosystem standpoint, integrating MAAI into digital marketplace workflows invites fundamental questions about the future of platform-mediated exchange, the evolution of human roles in digital networks, and the changing architecture of coordination across platform stakeholders. AI-based agents are increasingly able to execute complex, multi-step processes (e.g., matching supply and demand, orchestrating complementors, enabling service bundles), yet human involvement remains essential for oversight, governance, exception handling, and network orchestration (Liu et al., 2025). From the perspective of electronic markets, such modular and dynamically orchestrated agent architectures reshape how digital platforms coordinate transactions, mediate markets, structure networked business relationships, and enable value creation across ecosystem participants. Embedding agentic architectures into organizational workflows and platform infrastructures means embedding agency, negotiation, and contextual reasoning into the core of marketplace logic. Dynamic orchestration (C3) thus requiresmechanismsthatdynamicallyallocatetasksbetween human and AI-based agents depending on context, network conditions, value contribution, and ecosystem position (Mu et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025). In multi-sided platform settings, this may involve dynamic matching of human experts, automated agents, and complementary services within the marketplace ecosystem (Hemmer et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025). The intersection of MAAI and process mining offers a promising avenue in platform contexts: process mining can reconstruct real-time workflows from event logs (e.g., platform transaction logs, agent interaction logs, ecosystem partner activities), making visible how AI-based agents

From an organizational perspective, deploying MAAI within platforms is expected to create new forms of work (e.g., platform-orchestration roles, agent supervision roles), transform existing roles (e.g., marketplace operations, service provisioning, complementor coordination), and alter how value is created and captured across ecosystem participants. Strategic implementation thus requires not only technical integration but also platform governance, network orchestration, workforce strategy, ecosystem alignment, and business model adaptation. Finally, the economic viability of MAAI in platform contexts is closely tied to resource efficiency, cost-benefit of orchestration mechanisms, scalability of agent ecosystems, network effects, and value capture across platform participants. ## **_Study_ socio-technical implications (Components C4–C5)** 

The socio-technical implications of MAAI extend deeply into digital platforms: as AI-based agents assume increasingly complex decision-making roles in workflows and platform ecosystems, these developments bear on labor market transformations within the platform economy, platform governance, and value distribution across ecosystem actors, sustainability of digital networks, trust, and accountability in marketplace interactions. Within platform contexts, automation may democratize access to expert capabilities, but also risk reinforcing power asymmetries between platform owners, complementors, and users, applying downward pressure on wages, eroding returns for generic skill sets, or altering how value is shared in multi-sided markets (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2020; Agrawal et al., 2023; Dwivedi et al., 2023). From the perspective of networked business, preserving human responsibility and accountability becomes increasingly challenging when agent interactions are embedded in workflows and transactions. Faulty agent outputs may propagate across workflows, affecting, for example, matching outcomes, pricing, trust among actors, and overall ecosystem performance. Transparent evaluation and governance structures are essential for maintaining accountability, fairness, and trust in networked business settings (German Ethics Council, 2023; Ray, 2023; Schemmer et al., 2023). The design of interaction interfaces (C5) becomes critical in marketplace ecosystems: in agent-integrated workflows (C4) where tasks and agent configurations evolve on-thefly, platform interfaces must provide transparency to human stakeholders (e.g., workers, buyers, sellers, complementors, platform operators) without inducing cognitive overload. Long agent dialogues or agent networks may obscure rather than clarify decision logic, which threatens appropriate reliance and user trust in marketplace settings (Ray, 2023; Schemmer et al., 2023). # **Conclusion and research directions** 

MAAI builds on decades of research in MAS, process automation, and agentic IS, while extending these traditions into a new technological and organizational context shaped by FM and data-centric AI. By conceptualizing MAAI as a five-layer architecture— _foundation model (C1), data-centric perception and action (C2), dynamic orchestration (C3), agent-integrated workflow (C4),_ and _interaction interface (C5)_ —we have articulated how cognitive capabilities, coordination mechanisms, and human-AI interfaces interlock in ensembles of AI-based agents. This layered view situates MAAI as more than a rebranding of established concepts: it clarifies how recent advances enable flexible orchestration of knowledge work that is adaptive, socio-technical, and organizationally embedded. Our framework delineates three interdependentpathwaysforfutureresearch.Onthetechnical level, MAAI requires advances in agent modularity, orchestration logics, and validation methods that ensure reliability and robustness when workflows are dynamically composed from opaque FMs. On the organizational level, MAAI opens questions about hybrid human-AI work arrangements, shifting occupational roles, value distribution, and how methods such as process mining can make emergent agentic workflows transparent and optimizable. On the socio-technical level, MAAI raises fundamental concerns of accountability, fairness, and governance, demanding mechanisms that sustain trust and responsible oversight when autonomy and agency are distributed across multiple actors. 

By integrating these perspectives, the IS community can shape MAAI not simply as a technical evolution, but as a paradigmatic development in the design of digital systems— one that redistributes intelligence and coordination across human and computational agents. Grounded in its conceptual roots yet attuned to emerging challenges, this perspective positions MAAI as a foundation for cumulative, interdisci- 

plinary research into the future of knowledge work, digital markets, and socio-technical systems.
